# Summary

Ceramic material makes up a huge percentage of the material recovered and analyzed by archaeologists, often regarded as the most common of archaeological materials [@Orton:2013, p. 3]. This abundance presents a challenge for analysis, because far more is collected than can be adequately analyzed [@Velde:1999, pp. 1-2]. `AMFOrA` (Automatic Macroscopic Fabric and Orientation Analyzer) is a Python package that automatically analyzes pottery scans taken on a flatbed scanner and returns raw data that can be used to create distinct quantitative fingerprints of the internal makeup of broken fragments that would normally go unused.

# Statement of need

Ceramic artifacts are among the most important vestiges of the past that archaeologists can study due to their ubiquity, durability, and vast range of interpretable qualities. For over 20,000 years humans have used fired ceramics in some form or another, creating pots, tools, and art. The technology is also nearly globally ubiquitous, as it does not require special geologic or environmental conditions. Ceramic artifacts begin their lives as clay- malleable, but impermanent and degradable. Once fired, these forms are rendered permanent [@Heimann:2010]. They are fragile and brittle, but they will not biodegrade. Clay’s malleability allows for the formation of countless useful objects for storage, trade, cooking and more [@Haeberlin:1919; @Henig:1983; @Peacock:1991]. Potters’ decisions are stored in the clay forever- the thickness of a cooking vessel or the selection of inclusions to change a vessel’s porosity are conscious decisions in the potter’s mind that produce known results. Further, meaning and symbolism can be expressed through this medium [e.g., @Campbell:2010]. Ornamentation shows a deeper relationship between the potter and their work that shines a light on social relations at a broader scale than the individual pot’s utility or potter’s skill [e.g., @Plog:1978; @Plog:1980; @Plog:1983]. Different forms can mean different things, but there are numerous ways to impart meaning into a vessel, including but not limited to glaze, incisions, carvings, paint, and firing environment. As there is such a depth of meaning in such ostensibly simple artifacts, numerous techniques have been developed by archaeologists to work back to the broader social landscape to which these vessels originally belonged. Not all archaeology has the luxury of dealing with full vessels from which form, glaze, incisions, and paint can be easily seen. In survey archaeology, the ceramic fragments (sherds) we encounter have sat on the surface of the soil for hundreds if not thousands of years, and are usually so worn and fragmented that deriving their original form or decoration is impossible. However, their internal matrix, composed of clay and bits of added temper or inclusions, can tell archaeologists a great deal about how or why a vessel was made [@Shepard:1985, pp. 156-157], and macroscopic fabric analysis has a strong track record in survey contexts of precisely this kind [@Moody:2003]. While "high-tech" methods exist: e.g., thin-section petrography [@Peterson:2009; @Quinn:2022], X-Ray Fluorescence, Laser Ablation Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry, and Neutron Activation Analysis [@Dias:2007], that produce strong petrographic or compositional data, they are often cost-prohibitive to the point that few sherds can be studied in depth [@Rice:2015]. On the other hand, macroscopic approaches involving a careful eye and a magnifying loupe can increase the number of sherds analyzed at the cost of the depth of information recovered.

`AMFOrA` represents a modern approach to this problem by leveraging open-source computer-vision tools to improve data extraction and analysis at a fraction of the cost of the aforementioned "high-tech" methods. Researchers can now provide scanned images of a polished sherd face to `AMFOrA` and obtain a tidy dataframe or CSV in which each sherd is a row and each extracted index is a column [@Harris:2020; @McKinney:2010; @pandas:2020]. These indices are numerous but include sherd area, inclusion and void counts and areas, orientation of inclusions within the paste, paste color, inclusion color, and firing interpretations based on color differentials across the sherd. `AMFOrA` also includes rudimentary multivariate statistical tools that allow researchers to see which ceramics cluster together, creating typologies based on the numerous macroscopic data points extracted by the analysis routines [@Pedregosa:2011; @Virtanen:2020]. These tools offer k-means [@MacQueen:1967], hierarchical [@Ward:1963], and density-based clustering, with cluster validity assessed by silhouette analysis [@Rousseeuw:1987]. Results are visualized through biplots, dendrograms, and other diagnostic plots [@Hunter:2007; @Plotly:2015].

# State of the field

Traditional macroscopic analysis is done by visual inspection of sherds, sometimes with the assistance of a 20x magnifying loupe. More recently, archaeologists have used Dinolite microscopes with proprietary software to annotate images on a case-by-case basis. General-purpose open-source image-analysis tools such as OpenCV [@Bradski:2000] and ImageJ [@Schneider:2012] exist, and digital image analysis has been applied to ceramic thin sections [@Livingood:2009], but these tools are not aimed directly at ceramic analysis and require separate expertise.

The process of sorting and typing large collections through macroscopic analysis is notably time-consuming, relying heavily on individual expertise. Despite attempts at standardization, such as binning quantitative characteristics into ranged characteristics (i.e. low, medium, high inclusion count), subjectivity inevitably introduces bias [@Velde:1999, p. 233; @WintherJacobsen:2008, p. 50]. The absence of standardized terminologies for broader archaeological comparisons in large survey databases is a persistent issue, impacting consistency and accessibility [@Bevan:2013; @Maritan:2019, p. 5086]. Often, macroscopic determinations are done in the field and not reassessed during traditional post-field-work analysis, relying on matching a predetermined fabric group [@Dasckiewicz:2009, p. 29]. While other efforts are underway to automate data collection through image analysis software, challenges persist in fully automating the process, often necessitating ongoing calibration and oversight from experts [@AlberoSantacreu:2017; @Frahm:2008; @Velde:1999]. `AMFOrA` addresses these gaps by quantifying each textural element in its own column, either as a tally or a measurement, forgoing the need for simplification and offering direct comparability. It introduces bias only as a repeatable miscalculation of pixel values, rather than deteriorating through fatigue or lack of interest, and issues surrounding color are mitigated by a uniform scanning environment and a three-dimensional color space (HSV).

# Software design

`AMFOrA` is built on the OpenCV computer-vision library [@Bradski:2000] and detects non-plastic inclusions and voids through two independent methods, blob detection and contour detection, each operating on different computational principles with distinct strengths and limitations. By running both and reporting their results in separate column sets, `AMFOrA` lets researchers evaluate the convergence between methods and select the output best suited to their analytical question. The pipeline was initially implemented in scikit-image [@vanderWalt:2014] but moved to OpenCV, which provided a faster pipeline with superior results.

Blob detection identifies regions that differ from their surroundings in brightness or color [@Lindeberg:1998; @Szeliski:2022]. As implemented in OpenCV's `SimpleBlobDetector`, the algorithm applies binary thresholds at incremental intensity levels, identifies connected components at each level, and retains features whose center remains stable across many thresholds; transient features appearing at only one or two levels are discarded, giving the method robust resistance to noise [@Lindeberg:1998]. This makes it well suited to fine-grained pastes where micro-inclusions are difficult to distinguish from background. Its principal limitation is geometric: each feature is characterized as a circle, so while counts and size rankings are reliable, area measurements for elongated or irregular inclusions and voids should be treated as approximations [@Szeliski:2022].

Contour detection takes the opposite approach, searching for boundaries where intensity changes abruptly. It begins with Canny edge detection [@Canny:1986], traces the resulting edges as continuous curves using a border-following algorithm [@Suzuki:1985], and returns closed curves as contours whose exact perimeters yield area, aspect ratio, and other geometric properties. Because each boundary is traced as a polygon rather than approximated as a circle, contour detection accurately captures the true shape of angular fragments, elongated mineral laths, and branching voids, making it the preferred method for granulometric and morphological analysis. Its limitation is the inverse of blob detection's: edge-based methods achieve high sensitivity at the cost of specificity [@Szeliski:2022], so paste-matrix variation and imaging artifacts can produce spurious contours. `AMFOrA` therefore applies a multi-stage shape-quality filter based on aspect ratio, solidity, and compactness to remove these false positives, with more permissive thresholds for voids, whose organic-burnout morphology is genuinely more irregular than that of mineral inclusions.

# Research impact statement

`AMFOrA` is at the cutting edge of archaeological software development. Because of the specific use case for such software, it has not yet become a common workflow among archaeologists. An initial article describing its utility is currently in review, in which it is used to create typologies of ceramics from Mallorca, Spain that align with the distinctions a trained human ceramicist would make [@Iacobucci:2026]. The software has also been validated through experimental ceramic briquettes of known temper composition: 150 samples of known granulometric size and distribution were run through the software as a ground-proofing test of its ability to extract meaningful results, and these were compared against computer-generated ceramic images in which the exact number and location of inclusions were known. Beyond its use in research, `AMFOrA` is well placed to serve as an intermediate step that helps surveyors and ceramicists decide which samples warrant costly high-tech analysis, while still yielding a wealth of typological information on its own. A course on ceramic analysis built around the software is also being designed, where its low cost and quantitative output make it a useful tool for introducing students to fabric analysis.
